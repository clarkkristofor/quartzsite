
_by [[Brian McLaren]]_

When I first met Fr. Richard Rohr around 1999, I felt that he and I were on a similar journey, though we came from very different starting points, and he was many steps ahead of me on the path.  

I was coming from a Protestant/Evangelical background, and Fr. Richard was coming from a Roman Catholic background, but I felt we were both trying to sort through the versions of Christianity we had inherited, and we were trying to figure out what was essential, what was most truly important for present and future generations. 

Whenever I saw that we were speaking at the same event or happened to be in the same city, I’d reach out and see if we could get dinner, which we did several times. Once, over dinner in a little Mexican restaurant in San Diego, Fr. Richard shared with me some things that really clicked for me. He said, “It seems like you and I are traveling around the country trying to help people accept some new ideas, but the problem is, many people’s minds aren’t open to these ideas. It’s not that they won’t accept them; it’s that they _can’t_ accept them because these ideas require a new way of seeing, a new way of thinking, a new mind. They can’t accept new ideas until they have a new mind.” Then he talked to me about the contemplative mind, the nondual mind, and I felt like a thousand pennies dropped. 

Several years after that, in 2015, I was writing a book called _The Great Spiritual Migration_. I discovered that the Buddhist teacher Thich Nhat Hanh had also been on a similar journey on the other side of the world, decades earlier and in a very different religious tradition. As a Buddhist born in Viet Nam in 1926, Thich Nhat Hanh came of age during a period when his fellow Buddhists were being pulled toward civil war, a war rooted in the legacy of colonialism. In 1955, when the Viet Nam War broke out, the Buddhism of the Vietnamese didn’t seem to influence their behavior in ways one might hope.  

For many, it seemed, their religion was about the quest for private peace of mind or bliss for a devoted few, but it lacked coherent social and political impact. Thich Nhat Hanh and some of his peers felt that they could not simply stay in their monasteries, carrying on a form of disengaged Buddhism as their country was being torn apart. They needed to engage mindfully with the world around them, a world on fire. 

Of course, this felt very familiar to me as I regarded Christianity across our history. For all our Christian piety, for all our Reformations, Great Awakenings, and revivals, for all our churches and seminaries and (more recently) religious radio and TV offerings, our history was full of witch hunts, colonization, religious wars, gender domination, land theft, genocide, slavery, apartheid, gross economic injustice, and ecological destruction.  

In the mid 1960s, Thich Nhat Hanh quietly formed the Order of Interbeing. He invited six original members, three men and three women. He taught them fourteen principles of what he called Engaged Buddhism. The group allowed no new members for many years. They were testing a concept (and they were surviving the dislocation of the war). Now, the order has grown to include thousands. 

Here's how the Order of Interbeing articulates and seeks to embody the Fourteen Precepts or Mindfulness Trainings that have shaped their community: 

_The Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings are the very essence of the Order of Interbeing. They are the torch lighting our path, the boat carrying us, the teacher guiding us. They allow us to touch the nature of interbeing in everything that is and to see that our happiness is not separate from the happiness of others. Interbeing is not a theory; it is a reality that can be directly experienced by each of us at any moment in our daily lives. The Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings help us cultivate concentration and insight which free us from fear and the illusion of a separate self._ 

The Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings 

1. OPENNESS: Do not be idolatrous about or bound to any doctrine, theory, or ideology, even Buddhist ones. Buddhist systems of thought are guiding means; they are not absolute truth. 
    
2. NON-ATTACHMENT TO VIEWS: Do not think the knowledge you presently possess is changeless, absolute truth. Avoid being narrow-minded and bound to present views. Learn and practice nonattachment from views in order to be open to receive others’ viewpoints. Truth is found in life and not merely in conceptual knowledge. Be ready to learn throughout your entire life and to observe reality in yourself and in the world at all times. 
    
3. FREEDOM OF THOUGHT: Do not force others, including children, by any means whatsoever, to adopt your views, whether by authority, threat, money, propaganda, or even education. However, through compassionate dialogue, help others renounce fanaticism and narrowness. 
    
4. AWARENESS OF SUFFERING: Do not avoid contact with suffering or close your eyes before suffering. Do not lose awareness of the existence of suffering in the life of the world. Find ways to be with those who are suffering, including personal contact, visits, images, and sounds. By such means, awaken yourself and others to the reality of suffering in the world. 
    
5. COMPASSiONATE, HEALTHY LIVING: Do not accumulate wealth while millions are hungry. Do not take as the aim of your life fame, profit, wealth, or sensual pleasure. Live simply and share time, energy, and material resources with those who are in need. 
    
6. TAKING CARE OF ANGER: Do not maintain anger or hatred. Learn to penetrate and transform them when they are still seeds in your consciousness. As soon as they arise, turn your attention to your breath in order to see and understand the nature of your hatred. 
    
7. DWELLING HAPPILY IN THE PRESENT MOMENT: Do not lose yourself in dispersion and in your surroundings. Practice mindful breathing to come back to what is happening in the present moment. Be in touch with what is wondrous, refreshing, and healing both inside and around you. Plant seeds of joy, peace, and understanding in yourself in order to facilitate the work of transformation in the depths of your consciousness. 
    
8. TRUE COMMUNITY AND COMMUNICATION: Do not utter words that can create discord and cause the community to break. Make every effort to reconcile and resolve all conflicts, however small. 
    
9. TRUE AND LOVING SPEECH: Do not say untruthful things for the sake of personal interest or to impress people. Do not utter words that cause division and hatred. Do not spread news that you do not know to be certain. Do not criticize or condemn things of which you are not sure. Always speak truthfully and constructively. Have the courage to speak out about situations of injustice, even when doing so may threaten your own safety. 
    
10. PROTECTING AND NOURISHING THE SANGHA (COMMUNITY): Do not use the Buddhist community for personal gain or profit or transform your community into a political party. A religious community, however, should take a clear stand against oppression and injustice and should strive to change the situation without engaging in partisan conflicts. 
    
11. RIGHT LIVELIHOOD: Do not live with a vocation that is harmful to humans and nature. Do not invest in companies that deprive others of their chance to live. Select a vocation that helps realize your ideal of compassion. 
    
12. REVERENCE FOR LIFE: Do not kill. Do not let others kill. Find whatever means possible to protect life and prevent war. 
    
13. GENEROSITY: Possess nothing that should belong to others. Respect the property of others, but prevent others from profiting from human suffering or the suffering of other species on Earth. 
    
14. TRUE LOVE: Do not mistreat your body. Learn to handle it with respect. Do not look on your body as only an instrument. Preserve vital energies (sexual, breath, spirit) for the realization of the Way. (For brothers and sisters who are not monks and nuns:) Sexual expression should not take place without love and commitment. In sexual relationships, be aware of future suffering that may be caused. To preserve the happiness of others, respect the rights and commitments of others. Be fully aware of the responsibility of bringing new lives into the world. Meditate on the world into which you are bringing new beings.

I realized this is exactly what Richard Rohr had been doing in the CAC. The integration of action _and_ contemplation meant inviting people into an _engaged spirituality_. This spirituality wasn’t simply different doctrines or ideas or structures. It was a different way of thinking, of training ourselves to see the world and engage with it. It was an alternative orthodoxy that was more interested in a way of life than a system of belief or hierarchy.  

Of course, at that time, I had no idea that a few years later I would come to work more closely with Fr. Richard and the CAC, leading to this moment of writing these thoughts for this last module in our first trimester as co-learners and companions on this journey. 

So, here we find ourselves in the 2020s, almost a hundred years after Thich Nhat Hanh was born. The engaged contemplative spirituality of Thich Nhat Hanh and Richard Rohr is more needed than ever.  

Still today, many religious and spiritual communities offer little more than an evacuation plan (helping people prepare to evacuate a dying earth for heaven). Others offer an isolation plan (helping people withdraw into little bubbles of serenity while the world around them burns up and falls apart).  

But engaged contemplation offers a transformation plan, where we take seriously our own inner transformation as inseparably related to and engaged with transformation in our hot-mess world. 

Back in 2007, I had written a book about our hot-mess world called _Everything Must Change_. I tried to understand what our biggest global crises were and how they were interconnected. I talked about four crises: 

1. _Planet_ (Overshoot): We are sucking out more resources and pumping out more waste products than the world can sustain. This condition is caused “overshoot.” Human civilization has exceeded the carrying capacity of the planet, and this creates a wide range of effects, including global warming/boiling, soil depletion, fisheries collapse, species extinction, ice melt, sea–current collapse, and more. 
    
2. _Poverty_ (Economic Injustice): Our global economic and political systems funnel a larger and larger percentage of wealth and power to a smaller and smaller percentage of global elites. This inequality leads to suffering, social unrest, and instability on many levels. 
    
3. _Peace_ (Violence, Race, Hostility): In the presence of an unstable climate and gross inequalities of wealth and power, conflicts grow—racial and religious conflicts; conflicts resulting from mass migration, petty crime, and organized crime; conflicts worsened by political corruption and authoritarianism; and conflicts intensified by the proliferation of weapons of all kinds, including weapons of mass destruction. 
    
4. _Spirit_: Normally, our religious or spiritual traditions would help us unify and deal with the previous three crises, but our religious institutions are fragmented and incapable of helping us. In fact, often, they are making things worse. 
    

Over fifteen years have passed since I finished _Everything Must Change_, and those four interrelated crises are still raging across our global civilization. Our leaders—religious and political—are still so often deeply in denial. They are distracted with symptoms rather than root causes. So few are engaged and so many are disengaged. 

Thankfully, though, more and more are waking up, coming alive, and getting engaged. (For example, check out what many of the world’s religious leaders affirmed about the ecological crisis when they gathered recently in Chicago: [https://religionnews.com/2023/08/17/faith-leaders-call-for-repentance-and-spiritual-reformation-to-address-climate-change](https://religionnews.com/2023/08/17/faith-leaders-call-for-repentance-and-spiritual-reformation-to-address-climate-change) and [https://climaterepentance.com/the-spiritual-principles/)](https://climaterepentance.com/the-spiritual-principles/)) 

You are in the Living School because you are part of this process of waking up, coming alive, and getting engaged. You share the sense that a deep transformation of spirit is necessary—engaged contemplative spirituality in diverse expressions, both to help us survive the troubles that lie ahead and to sow the seeds for a new way of life on the other side of these troubles. (That’s the focus of my next writing project that will be published in 2024.) 

Over these years, I have come to see God very differently than I used to. God is no longer the Supreme Being outside or above the universe who occasionally (as Barbara Brown Taylor aptly put it)  reaches down to stir it with a stick. Instead, I experience God as the divine love that resides in my own heart, in the hearts of all people, and, in a real sense, in and through all creation as well. I recall that my friend and Franciscan Sister Ilia Delio tried to capture this “fantastic mystery of love” in a talk she gave for the CAC’s Conspire conference back in 2014: 

> Everything that exists speaks of God, reflects that love energy of God. But God is more than anything that exists. God is always the more of our lives. We can’t contain God. If we try to control God, that’s not God; God always spills over our lives. So, God is our future. If we’re longing for something we desire, it’s that spilled-over love of our lives that’s pulling us onward, that’s luring us into something new. But we don’t trust this God of implanted desire often. We were pretty sure that God’s there, and we’re here, and we just need to keep on the straight and narrow path. . . . 
> 
> What Francis of Assisi recognized is God is in every direction that you might arrive, you might not arrive. You might arrive late; you might arrive early. _It’s not the arrival that counts._ _It’s God!_ It’s not the direction that counts. It’s just being there, trusting that you will be going where God wants you. In other words, God is with us. Every step of the way is God-empowered love energy. But we tend to break down and start controlling things: “If I go this way, I’m going to get lost. Well, what if it’s wrong? What _will_ happen to me?” Well, what will happen to you? Something will happen. But guess what? Something’s going to happen whether or not you go; that’s the whole point of life. So, it’s all about love. 
> 
> So, it’s not like we’ve got this, “Here’s God; here’s us. God’s just waiting till we get our act together and then we’ll all be well.” That’s a boring God. That’s not even God. God is alive. God is love. Love is pulling us on to do new things, and we need to trust the power of God in our lives to do new things. . . . We need to unwire ourselves to recognize that the God of Jesus Christ is, you might say, the power beneath our feet, the depth of the beauty of everything that exists, and the future into which we are moving. . . . 
> 
> Every one of us is written in the heart of God from all eternity, born into the stars; born, you might say, into the galaxies; born on this earth in small forms, developing and coming to explicit form in our lives, given a name. It’s a fantastic mystery of love. 

There is so much to be done in a world on fire. It might seem that we don’t have time to draw deep from the well of Spirit, from the mystery of love. But I’d say the reverse is true. We don’t have time to waste rushing headlong into reactive action without first learning to center down and engage with the Spirit—empowering us, guiding us, sustaining us, expressing love through us, in us, and as us. I suppose you could say it’s a dual engagement: engaging with God and with God’s beautiful, beloved world, a world on fire.
