# Great Love and Great Suffering Offer Great Wisdom

If contemplation is deep listening and not deep ignoring, or if contemplation is a long, loving look at the real, sometimes what we see in that reality is deep suffering. And a long, loving look at suffering can be an initiation and an invitation.  

This week, we explore the lives of two spiritual teachers, the Buddha and St. Francis, who courageously walked their own paths of descent. They offer us examples and guidance into the connection between great suffering, great love, and great wisdom as we explore the insights of both Christian and Buddhist traditions in conversation with one another.  

Over 500 years before Jesus walked the earth, Siddhartha, a wealthy tribal prince living in India in a time of upheaval, took a long, hard look at the suffering around him. This suffering would shake him awake, causing him to ask hard questions and ultimately set out on a quest for answers and enlightenment, not just for himself, but to bring medicine to the world.  

In our **_Deep Listening_** this week, Zen teacher Roshi Joan Halifax tells us Siddhartha's "father was told by a kind of seer that the Buddha would be either a king or a spiritual teacher. And, needless to say, his father wanted him to be a king, so to speak, or the leader of the clan.” His father was informed by this seer that if the Buddha could be kept from seeing the suffering of the world, then he would in fact become a great ruler and not a reformer. 

So, his father set out to keep him ignorant of the reality of the suffering of the world, protected in the pleasures and the privileges of the palace. But, despite his best efforts, his son managed to sneak out and witness “the four passing signs” of old age, disease, death, and poverty. 

Roshi Joan tells us, “The vision of fragility, of vulnerability, of mortality, the truth of suffering, woke him up. He was like, ‘I actually need to move out into the world. I need to awaken to the lived experience of the truth.'”  

Seventeen hundred years later in Italy, the son of a wealthy merchant in Assisi would face similar confrontations with suffering—war, imprisonment, poverty, and disease. He too would be shaken to his core and then awaken to shine a light for a corrupted culture in disrepair. 

Despite the best efforts of his father, Francis would renounce his wealth to seek a different path. He would become a prophet, a teacher, an advocate and ally of the poor, and an ecumenical voice of peace who crossed the battlefields of the Crusades to meet with Islamic leaders. To this day, St. Francis invites mainstream Christianity to wake up again to the love at its core. As Fr. Richard Rohr tells us in his conversation with Roshi Joan, "Much of what we later came to know of as the Christian religion became [all about] ‘salvation from sin.’ Francis, however, was not sin-centered. Instead, he was suffering- and love-centered. This approach creates a different notion of religion. It becomes less about religion itself and more about learning how to be human.... St. Francis, being a thirteenth-century Catholic, wouldn’t have used the word _enlightenment_, although he doesn’t use the word _salvation_ either, really.  He was more about being liberated for love...the love of everything.”  

As we listen this week, let us consider the ways that dominant culture (personified in the fathers of both Siddhartha and Francis) has tried to keep us ignorant of the reality of suffering in the world, including the suffering of others and in our own hearts. How has this kept us from embracing the path of descent?
