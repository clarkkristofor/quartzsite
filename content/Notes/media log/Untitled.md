**Best Novel:**  
_(A work of science fiction or fantasy fiction longer than 40,000 words)_

- [Private Rites](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250344311/privaterites/) by Julia Armfield – It’s always a bit of a struggle for me, whether to nominate literary SF novels that are unquestionably among the best work of the last year but have no chance of making it onto the ballot (it’s for this reason that I’m forgoing nominations for the equally deserving [In Ascension](https://wrongquestions.blogspot.com/2024/02/recent-reading-in-ascension-by-martin.html) and [Cahokia Jazz](https://wrongquestions.blogspot.com/2024/03/cahokia-jazz-by-francis-spufford.html)). But Armfield’s second novel is too good not to recognize. A family drama set in a climate changed future, it persuasively argues for climate fiction’s place not just within science fiction, but horror. ([review](https://wrongquestions.blogspot.com/2024/12/recent-reading-private-rites-by-julia.html))
- [The Ministry of Time](https://www.simonandschuster.com/books/The-Ministry-of-Time/Kaliane-Bradley/9781668045145) by Kaliane Bradley – It’s been months since I picked up Bradley’s debut expecting a disposable time travel romcom and discovered a sharp and disquieting meditation on immigration, refugeeism, and how the language of inclusion and social progress can conceal a naked desire to regress towards open colonial expansion. These are, of course, all issues that feel all the more pressing in the present moment, and make this excellent novel feel even more impactful. ([review](https://wrongquestions.blogspot.com/2024/06/recent-reading-ministry-of-time-by.html))
- [Exordia](https://us.macmillan.com/books/9781250233011/exordia/) by Seth Dickinson – At once core science fiction and full of gonzo inventiveness, Dickinson’s mind-bending novel delivers one of the most plausible, and terrifying, alien invasion stories I’ve ever read, featuring an utterly original alien species. It then proceeds with a tale of perhaps-doomed resistance, in the process of which it invents whole new branches of physics and mathematics, even as the characters debate morality and try to decide how, and whether, they can save the world. If the Hugos don’t recognize a novel like this, I don’t know what they’re even for.
- [The Book of Love](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/239722/the-book-of-love-by-kelly-link/) by Kelly Link – In her first novel after a quarter century as America’s doyenne of weird, sardonic, pop culture-inflected short stories, Link preserves many of those same qualities, as she follows the misadventures of three teens who return home after being absent (and in fact dead) for a year, and must protect themselves and their town from the predations of magical creatures locked in an ancient enmity. Though its baggy, meandering quality can sometimes be a challenge, it eventually comes to feel like the essence of the novel, as its heroes become distracted by town history, personal relationships, and just weird asides that are the very things that have made Link such a beloved author.
