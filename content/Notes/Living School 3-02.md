## Introduction 
The “and” between action and contemplation is just one of the many ways that “both/and” thinking is a crucial part of walking the path of love.    

The importance of the “yes/and” is praised and cultivated as a crucial facet of creativity, in everything from improv comedy to contact improv dance. And yet, there is an even deeper aspect to “both/and.”  Author and Living School Alumna Felicia Murrell defines “and” simply as “The Restorative Power of Love in an Either/Or World.”  

In The Naked Now, Richard Rohr confesses, “I have been told that I have a fixation for trying to see almost all things as both/and—or as a ‘collision of opposites.’” In fact, this is a big part of how the path of descent leads to the path of love, how we are transformed through great suffering and great love when we face these collisions in ourselves and the world around us.  

Carl Jung described this as “nothing less than a crucifixion of the ego, its agonizing suspension between irreconcilable opposites” (CW 9.ii). When we are crucified by a catch-22 or “conflicts of duty,” we experience the most growth, for we die and are reborn. 

Early Christian scholar Origen of Alexandria tells us that it is colliding with the skandala—the roadblocks, the impossibilities, and the contradictions in life—that drives us to find deeper, more worthy divine meanings. 

Last week, Fr. Richard described the process by which “you hit up against something that makes you turn a little different direction. That is a recipe for ongoing growing up. Those kind of people...are people who faced seeming contradictions and allowed them to be a non-contradiction in them.” 

We allow these seeming contradictions to become paradoxes.  

Fr. Richard writes: “Reality is paradoxical. If we are honest, everything is a clash of contradictions.” (TNN 145).   

Origen tells us that at first, facing these paradoxical tensions is dizzying and painful: “In this matter human intelligence is feeble and limited, when it tries to understand…. There is this conflict in human thoughts and reasonings, and the soundest arguments on either side oppose and rebut one another, each bending the mind of the thinker in its own direction” (OFP 42). This clash of arguments can drive us to frustration and even anger, but it can also humble us, slow us down, and challenge us to see within larger and deeper frames of reference. In this way, through paradox, we can mature in divine wisdom. 

Fr. Richard defines paradox this way: “A paradox is something that initially appears to be inconsistent or contradictory but might not be a contradiction inside of a different frame or seen with a different eye” (146).  

So, this is about growing into a new perspective, a new way to see, a wider and deeper frame of reference in our long, loving look at the real.  

Fr. Richard elaborates: “The history of spirituality tells us that we must learn to accept paradoxes, or we will never love anything or see it correctly.” (144) “Each one of us must learn to live with paradox, or we cannot live peacefully or happily even a single day in our lives. In fact, we must even learn to love paradox, or we will never be wise, forgiving, or possessing the patience of good relationships.” (145) 

Facing paradoxes in ourselves allows us to spot and sympathize with the paradoxes in others and become more skilled at facing the paradoxes in the world, even letting paradox become a path to lifegiving wisdom for us.

## Practice
This week, we offer you two additional practice options to consider: 

First, we encourage you to lean into your own paradoxes and to ponder what it might look like to create a daily practice of embracing the “both/and” of many of our seeming contradictions.  

For example, what would it look like to create a daily practice of taking time to acknowledge and honor both gratitude and grief, both sorrow and celebrations, and both your critique of others and your compassion for them? 

Second, we offer you the opportunity to engage in the practice of visio divina using this icon of Christ Pantokrator, an icon that resides in the world’s oldest continuously functioning Christian monastery in the Sinai desert, St. Catherine's Monastery. 

As you gaze upon the icon below, you may notice that the two sides of the face of Christ are asymmetrical, with one side being serene, the other being severe. 

Some have taught that this symbolizes how the Christ figure holds many seeming contradictions together in balance, or in paradox. 

The serene side of Christ’s face offers sustaining love and comfort for us in our suffering, while the severe side of Christ’s face represents Divine heart break and grief over human suffering. 

The severe side of Christ’s face reveals divine anger in solidarity with us over human injustice, while the serene side offers unending love and forgiveness for human shortcomings. 

The serene side of Christ’s face represents the unquenchable power of love, while the severe side represents the total vulnerability of God. 

The severe side shows us a God who suffers and dies with us, while the serene side reveals the resurrected reality of love. 

Indeed, this icon represents the opposites in paradoxical tension that Fr. Richard describes in our reading this week:  

Jesus, the Christ, has come to teach us about life and about ourselves. Jesus, as the icon of Christ consciousness (1 Cor. 2:16), is the very template of total paradox: human yet divine, heavenly yet earthly, physical yet spiritual, possessing a male body yet a female soul, killed yet alive, powerless yet powerful, victim yet victor, failure yet redeemer, marginalized yet central, singular yet everyone, incarnate yet cosmic, nailed yet liberated, resolving the great philosophical problem of the one and the many. (TNN 147) 

As you gaze upon this icon, we invite you to let it open you to all the paradoxes that love makes room for in yourself, humanity, reality, and in the face of the Divine.
