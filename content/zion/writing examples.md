## Discernment

Susannah, my daughter, just finished middle school at Sudlow. For the eighth grade graduation ceremony, each student prepared a slide. Susannah’s slide was sparkly and pink, with pictures of friends, show choir, and musical theater. For her future plans, she wrote “graduate and go to college.” 

Over Pentecost / Memorial Day weekend, our family went camping. Around the campfire, I asked Susannah about her future plans. Where did she want to go to college? What did she want to study? As I listened, I encouraged her to consider this or that and shared a bit about my college choices and experiences. All of this, gently, because it’s early, and she is her own person, and the world has changed.

Discernment is the art of wise decision-making. Not many of us are discerning about college, but probably all of us are discerning about something. Life is about decisions; some we don’t even realize we’re making.

Making wise decisions is more than making the right choices. For example, any number of schools might be the “right" school for Susannah. And there is plenty to learn even from the “wrong” school. Mistakes are our teachers, not our enemies. For colleges, “right” and “wrong” are about fit and quality and making a worthy investment of time and money, not ethics. But even when our decisions are about ethics, our decision-making process matters as much as the outcome. Fear of getting it wrong can trip us up as easily as overconfidence.

The Holy Spirit makes us wise discerners. And that involves pushing us beyond the surface toward a deeper, more liberating way. Consider Jesus’ Sermon on the Mount. After reading it, the Wednesday morning bible study wondered: what problem was Jesus trying to solve? Scholar Warren Carter suggests it wasn’t a lack of knowledge of God’s will and what God requires or a lack of willingness to do it – as if Jesus intended to either scare or woo. The problem was a lack of discernment.

God’s ways are love for enemies and generosity toward all. God’s purpose is giving God’s life away in Jesus to usher in a kingdom for those whose spirits are crushed by poverty, those who hunger and thirst for justice and healing. Now go, live in that kingdom. Choose God’s loving purposes in every choice. Such is the Sermon on the Mount, and the foundation for discernment Jesus offers.

May you and Susannah ground your decision-making in your very personal experience of knowing God. That’s how the Gospel of John would say it, meaning not a mere head-trip or academic exercise. Knowing God in your soul, in your bones, is life, John says; life abundant and eternal. And you do know God, because Jesus lives and dwells among you.

Our hope of making wise decisions is rooted here, in Jesus the vine. We are the branches. So dwell deeply in Jesus’s love and mercy and cosmic safety, forgiveness, healing, and joy. Then wisdom will arise.

Thanks be to God.

Pastor Clark Olson-Smith

## Howard Thurman
In my sermon Sunday, I mentioned Howard Thurman when speaking about “inner authority,” a phrase of his. I want to share a bit more about him with you.

Howard Thurman was a pastor and more. His work is centered at the intersection of contemplative prayer and work for social justice. He found God in silence and in nature and the person of Jesus, sustaining him to resist the indignity of racism. Firstly, God showed him himself as God’s beloved, so he could reject the self-negating lies of white supremacy. Later, Thurman met Mahatma Gandhi in India in 1935. The encounter lit a fire in him to bring inspiration from Gandhi’s non-violent movement to free India from British colonial rule to free people from Jim Crow in the United States. Thurman founded an interracial congregation in New York City and began to write, becoming the spiritual father of the civil rights movement in the 1960s. Every time he marched, Martin Luther King, Jr. carried with him a copy of Thurman’s book, Jesus and the Disinherited. 

If you want to learn more about Howard Thurman, I recommend a short but powerful book surveying his life and work by Lerita Coleman Brown: What Makes You Come Alive: A Spiritual Walk with Howard Thurman. Below I’ll share a few quotations that most inspired my sermon Sunday.

	If a person knows what word he can use to address you so as to draw you off balance, he can always keep you at his mercy. The basis of inner togetherness, one's sense of inner authority, must never be at the mercy of factors in one's environment, however significant they may be. Nothing from outside a man can destroy him until he opens the door and lets it in.... Whatever determines how you feel on the inside controls in large part the destiny of your life.
	
	–Howard Thurman, Deep Is the Hunger

+

	Realizing we have never been disconnected from the Presence, we discover a God-created self that does not capitulate to coercion and provocation. …"A new self emerges and that new self becomes the center of the orientation of my life," Thurman observes in a sermon. "Love does that for us, for it inspires in an individual what was sleeping, relaxed sense of worth and value and meaning, and when this slumbering thing awakens, the kind of radiance that circulates through all the corridors of one's life makes the individual see in himself what he had never seen before. It is the discovery of a new center around which increasingly all of the details of life are more and more organized.”
	
	–Lerita Coleman Brown, What Makes You Come Alive

+

Thanks be to God for Thurman’s witness. And that of Lerita Coleman Brown, who died just this past December.

Pastor Clark Olson-Smith

## Let love be genuine

"Let love be genuine... Contribute to the needs of the saints." In this way, Paul encourages the church in Rome, as we hear this Sunday. It's also what I've experienced  from you all in these recent weeks. Thank you again.

This week, I came back to the office for the first time since my accident. I do not have much stamina yet. I take it day by day. I'm learning patience, and you all continue to be patient with my recovery too.

I will be glad to be with you in worship Sunday, celebrating life, life in Christ, and a savior who suffers with and for us. I have most certainly experienced Christ with me, even in my first memories after the accident. I trust you've experienced it too: when God's consolations come to us, we can rejoice in being alive and in belonging to a God of hope, forgiveness, and healing, even when life is at its worst.

This week, I also made the decision not to travel to Tanzania in September. Zion people will still go, marking the 25th anniversary of our companionship with Kirangare Lutheran Parish. It was a hard decision, and I will miss seeing our brothers and sisters. But I hear it from you, my family, my doctors, and my own body: "Don't push it."

Living life - honoring the gift that it is and cooperating with God's healing and love - takes discipline and discernment. Sometimes, saying no to things we want to say yes to, and at other times, saying yes to things we want to say no to. In this holiest of spirits, Jesus said yes to the cross for our sake. It is the mystery of faith that Jesus, by his abundant grace, has made the pattern of our life together.

And speaking of life together, this Sunday, Zion welcomes to worship our sisters and brothers from St. Mark Lutheran Church in Davenport. Another gracious gift, symbolized in a joint choir. Let love continue to be genuine.

Thanks be to God.

Pastor Clark Olson-Smith

## The First Companionship
Zion is a congregation of faithful, visionary people. This is clear in the story of the genesis of Zion's companionship with Kirangare Lutheran Parish in Tanzania. 

This month, we celebrate the 25th anniversary of the companionship. On Sunday, September 13, we will send Zion travelers to celebrate in person with our brothers and sisters. On Sunday, September 20, we will mark the anniversary and commit to a renewed companionship covenant. Throughout, we will tell stories.

I assumed the companionship started from the top, with the bishops here and there. Here, Zion is part of the Southeastern Iowa (SEIA) Synod of the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America. There, Kirangare Parish is part of the Pare Diocese of the Evangelical Lutheran Church in Tanzania. I thought their diocese and our synod connected, bishop-to-bishop, then the bishops invited congregations in.

No. This is not the true story. It's also not how the kingdom of God works. The last shall be first; the first, last. The kingdom is like little seeds that grow big and tiny yeast making the whole bread dough rise.

Our companionship and all the companionships between congregations in our synod and parishes in their diocese started with one Zion person: Joyce Smith. Joyce was not a bishop, not even a pastor. Joyce was a member of Zion; a leader already, I'm sure. God gave Joyce a vision while she read a magazine called *The Lutheran*. The article told the story of congregation to parish companionships, and Joyce knew Zion needed to be part of one. She rallied other Zion people to this vision. Then that group rallied others in other congregations in our synod.

Now there are dozens of companionships between Pare Diocese parishes and SEIA Synod congregations. Many are dormant, like ours was for a long time. Many are active. But the companionship between Zion and Kirangare Parish is the oldest. Ours was the first. A season of celebrating 25th anniversaries begins here in this corner of Davenport and in the four villages - Kiragare, Mpare, Idaru, and Marindi - where Kirangare Lutheran Parish has preaching points.

Because God did a new thing and chose Joyce and Zion to be the catalyst.

You can hear Joyce tell the story in her own words, in her video message to Kiragare Lutheran Parish. You can see it online or at Zion on September 20. Joyce herself never traveled to Tanzania, but she holds Kiragare people in her heart as deep as anyone. 

Zion is a congregation of faithful, visionary people, and it has been for a long time. Even now, our own Bishop Amy Current looks to Zion as a model for other congregations in the synod. Still, we lead, hand in hand with our brothers and sisters in Kiragare Lutheran Parish.

Thanks be to God.

Pastor Clark Olson-Smith
