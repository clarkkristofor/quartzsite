This church year is ending. Christ the King Sunday, November 22, marks the end of this church year, while the First Sunday in Advent, November 29 marks the beginning of the new church year. Here is another sign of our calling to be citizens of heaven. We keep time differently.

The Gospel of Matthew has been our companion this year. Most Sundays, that's where the gospel reading came from. So it seems fitting, as we hear it for the last time, to say farewell and remember its unique witness to Jesus.

For Matthew, to follow Jesus is to obey Jesus. More than any other gospel, Matthew focuses on Jesus's teaching, arranging material from Mark's gospel and its own unique material into five big sections of teaching. The first and most important begins with the Beatitudes. Just as Moses received God's law on a mountain, so also, on a mountain, Jesus teaches his disciples: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven."

So right from the start, this Sermon on the Mount offers nine blessings, not 10 commandments. How do you obey a blessing? You don't. You receive a blessing. And blessings of this magnitude, so out of sync with the world-as-it-is, change who you are. They change how you see God. They paradoxically shock and gentle you into living out of sync with the world and in sync with God's love and peace. So you become the blessing.

Matthew witnesses to blessing and trauma. It echoes with the anger and grief of the utterly destructive war with Rome, with the painful divorce between church and synagogue. We hear it in repeated phrases like "throw him into the outer darkness where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth." At the same time, Matthew sings of forgiveness and reconciliation and, as commentators Davies and Allison have said, of "the desperate need for love and peace to displace hate and vengeance." 

So I read those hard phrases as God's own weeping and gnashing of teeth, over those who choose disobedience, violence, and the loneliness of hatred. In Matthew, the cross stands in that very outer darkness as our hope of salvation. A cross of blessing: God's broken heart embracing our own broken hearts and carrying us to life beyond the brokenness. It's Matthew who says that at the bleak moment of the crucifixion, tombs opened (27:51-53)! 

As we are becoming this blessing ourselves, we are free to receive Matthew's commandments as gifts, blessed reminders that we and the world are more than our suffering and that we meet Jesus himself in the least of these. 

Hold on to Matthew as we turn to Mark in Advent, and when the holiday blues surface within you in these coming weeks. Only in Matthew does Jesus say, "Come to me, all you who are weary and carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest."

Thanks be to God.
